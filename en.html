How to read the chart

Important: The Tier level does not show whether a method is “good” or “bad”. It shows how strong the research design is for interpreting the effect as caused by the method.

The chart combines two things: approximate effect size on student learning and strength of evidence for causal interpretation. The x-axis shows standardized effect size, roughly corresponding to Cohen’s d or Hedges g. The y-axis shows a Slavin-inspired evidence hierarchy. Tier 1 means the strongest support, for example meta-analyses of randomized or strong intervention studies. Tier 6 means the weakest support, for example theory, expert judgement or case studies.

The background colours show strength of evidence: greener fields indicate stronger support for causal interpretation, while redder fields indicate weaker support. Bubble size roughly shows how much research exists behind the area. Diamonds mark methods from the Science of Learning and cognitive psychology, such as retrieval practice, spacing, worked examples and cognitive load. Circles mark broader school interventions and other teaching methods.

What is Cohen’s d?

Cohen’s d is a standardized measure of effect size. It describes the size of the difference between an intervention group and a comparison group, expressed in standard deviations. As a rough rule of thumb, d≈0.2 is often called a small effect, d≈0.5 a medium effect and d≈0.8 a large effect.

In education research, even d≈0.3–0.5 can be meaningful, especially when the effect is based on many studies, robust design and realistic school settings.

Method note: are all values directly from research?

No. Not all values in the chart are exact research values, and not all studies demonstrate causality. The chart is a discussion tool and a synthesis. Some points are close to published research summaries, while others are approximate judgements.

1. More directly research-based

Mainly EEF-based approaches and some meta-analyses. These values are the most firmly grounded, but EEF often reports effects as “months of additional progress”. Here, such information has been approximately translated into d/g.

Examples: metacognition, feedback, tutoring, phonics, reading comprehension, digital technology and professional learning communities/PLC.

2. Research-based but estimated

The methods have real research support, but the chart’s d/g value is an approximate typical value rather than an exact datapoint from one specific meta-analysis.

Examples: retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, worked examples, cognitive load, dual coding, explicit instruction and language-oriented subject teaching.

3. More judgement-based / weaker datapoints

These values should be interpreted most cautiously. Research may exist, but often as correlations, observations, theory or mixed studies where it is difficult to state a stable d-value.

Examples: relational competence, student influence, entrepreneurial learning, project-based learning and exact d-values for learning styles.

Special note on PLATO: PLATO should not be understood as a regular student-facing method in the same way as retrieval practice or tutoring. It is an observation protocol that can be used for feedback and professional development. Research supports that PLATO captures teaching qualities associated with student outcomes, but the evidence that PLATO-based feedback itself causes improved outcomes is more indirect. It is therefore placed as research-based but estimated, not as Tier 1–2.

Strength of evidence for causal interpretation

  1. Tier 1: Meta-analyses or systematic reviews of randomized or strong intervention studies. Strongest support for causal interpretation.
  2. Tier 2: Randomized controlled trials or other strong intervention research. Relatively strong support for causal interpretation.
  3. Tier 3: Quasi-experiments or mixed interventions. Can provide some support for causal interpretation, but with more possible sources of error.
  4. Tier 4: Longitudinal studies or mixed observational studies. Often show relationships over time, but not certain causality.
  5. Tier 5: Cross-sectional studies and weaker correlational evidence. Mainly show association, not cause.
  6. Tier 6: Expert opinion, theory, case studies or lack of robust support. Weakest support for causal interpretation.

Sources

Education Endowment Foundation – Teaching and Learning Toolkit

Main source for several broad school interventions, including feedback, metacognition, tutoring, phonics, digital technology and collaborative learning.

EEF Toolkit Guide

Used to understand how EEF links standardized effect size to “months of additional progress”.

Dunlosky et al. (2013), Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques

Central review for Science of Learning methods such as practice testing, distributed practice, interleaving, self-explanation and elaborative interrogation.

Karpicke & Roediger (2008), The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning

Experimental research showing the strength of retrieval practice/the testing effect for long-term learning.

Roorda et al. (2011/2017), Teacher–student relationships and student engagement/achievement

Meta-analyses/reviews of associations between teacher-student relationships, engagement and achievement.

Pashler et al. (2008), Learning Styles: Concepts and Evidence

Review often used as a key source for the lack of robust support for learning styles as a teaching principle.

Robert Slavin – Evidence-based education

Inspires the evidence hierarchy: meta-analyses of RCTs highest, followed by RCTs, quasi-experiments, longitudinal studies, cross-sectional studies and expert opinion.

Lomos, Hofman & Bosker (2011), Professional communities and student achievement

Meta-analysis of professional learning communities/professional communities and student achievement. Reports a small but significant effect, approximately d≈0.25.

EEF – Effective Professional Development

Guidance report on how teacher professional development can be designed to influence teaching quality and student outcomes.

Motivational interviewing for students in school settings – meta-analysis

Recent meta-analysis of school-based motivational interviewing. It includes 38 studies and 207 effect sizes. Used here as support that MI has a research base, but not as an exact d-value in the chart.

Strait et al. (2012), A Randomized Trial of Motivational Interviewing

Randomized study with 103 middle-school students where MI was tested to promote academic achievement.

Terry et al. (2014), Dosage Effects of Motivational Interviewing

Randomized study comparing one versus two MI sessions and reporting effects on grades in mathematics, science and history.

Strait et al. (2019), Classroom-Based Motivational Interviewing

Randomized study in higher education. The intervention group improved, but the difference compared with the control group was not significant, showing that the evidence is mixed.

Kullberg, Runesson Kempe & Marton (2017), Variation theory of learning

Open article on variation theory in mathematics teaching. Used here as support for what variation theory and Learning Study involve, but not as an exact effect size.

Björklund (2016), Challenges and virtues of theory-driven education

Meta-study on variation theory in early education. Supports that there is a research field, but does not provide a simple comparable d-value for the chart.

Stanford CSET – Protocol for Language Arts Teaching Observations (PLATO)

Describes PLATO as an observation tool for capturing qualities in language arts/ELA teaching and as support for professional development.

EdInstruments – PLATO instrument overview

Describes PLATO as an observation protocol for thirteen elements of ELA teaching, developed for studies of teaching practice and student outcomes.

Peters et al. (2021), Effects of Data-Based Decision-Making

Study on data-based decision-making reporting small positive effect trends for reading fluency and motivation. Used as support for a cautious DBDM placement.

Hoogland et al. – Data use professional development meta-analysis

Meta-analysis of data use/DBDM-related professional development. Reports a significant positive effect on student outcomes around 0.17, supporting a cautious DBDM placement.

Faber et al. (2023), Digital student monitoring tools

Best-evidence meta-analysis of digital student monitoring tools. Reports an overall effect around ES = 0.12 on student outcomes, with some combinations higher.

Grossman et al., Measure for Measure

Study of the relationship between different observation measures, including PLATO, and student outcomes. PLATO showed clearer associations with more ambitious language/reading comprehension tests than with simpler measures.

IES project on PLATO as a professional development tool

Describes research where PLATO is used for observation, feedback and professional development to improve ELA teaching and, ultimately, student outcomes.
